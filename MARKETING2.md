## First: what is a prediction market?

A prediction market lets people bet on whether a specific future event will
happen. "Will the Fed cut rates in March?" "Will this film gross over $100M
opening weekend?" You buy a share in *Yes* or *No*. If you're right, each share
pays out $1. If you're wrong, it pays nothing.

The price becomes a forecast. If *Yes* trades at 65 cents, the market is
collectively saying there's roughly a 65% chance. That's the appeal: a
continuously updating, money-backed probability, which often beats polls and
pundits.

The two biggest venues are **Polymarket** (crypto-based, global) and **Kalshi**
(US-regulated). The category has grown fast — billions of dollars of volume a
month — and now gets regular coverage in the WSJ, Bloomberg and CoinDesk.

## Where it breaks — and this is the whole business

For a market to pay out, somebody has to decide **what actually happened**.
That step is called **resolution**, and it is where the industry hurts.

Most markets resolve themselves. The team won or it didn't. The price closed
above the number or it didn't. The interesting failures are the ones where
*everyone agrees on the facts and still disagrees on the outcome* — because the
disagreement is about the **wording of the question**, not the world.

A real example. A market asked whether a company would **sell any Bitcoin by
31 May**. It turned out the company *did* sell some — between 26 and 31 May —
but only disclosed it in a filing on 1 June. So: did it happen "by 31 May"?

- The sale occurred before the deadline → resolve **Yes**
- Nobody could have known by the deadline → resolve **No**

Both readings are defensible. The rules never said which one governs. Tens of
millions of dollars sat on the answer, and it ended up decided by a vote.

**That is the problem Prema exists to solve.** Not fraud, not manipulation —
just sentences that don't say what they need to say, discovered too late.

### How disputes get settled today

Polymarket outsources resolution to something called an **oracle** — in its case
a protocol named **UMA**. Someone proposes an answer, and anyone can challenge
it by putting up a deposit. If they do, the question goes to a **vote of token
holders**, who decide the outcome.

You don't need to know how any of that works technically. What matters
commercially is: *the referees are anonymous token holders voting, with no
precedent database, no structured evidence, and no independent appeal.* When
serious money is involved, that is not a satisfying answer — and traders have
started suing venues over it.

## What Prema is

Prema is building the **neutral resolution layer** for that hard tail of cases.
Three things:

1. **A dispute-risk score** — read a market's rules *before* you trade and flag
   the wording likely to cause a fight.
2. **An evidence and precedent engine** — when a market *is* contested, assemble
   the timestamped sources, the relevant past cases, and a recommended outcome.
3. **An appeals desk** — an independent, auditable second opinion.

The hard constraint, and the whole moat: **we never run a market, take a
position, or rule on anything.** We publish evidence and probabilities, show our
own accuracy in public, and let others decide. Neutrality isn't a value
statement here — it's the product.

**Stage:** pre-launch, pre-funding, one technical founder. Currently building a
public dataset of every Polymarket resolution since 2024, plus a page publishing
how accurate our risk score turns out to be. **Nothing gets sold before that
accuracy page is live.** Credibility is the asset; claiming results we haven't
demonstrated would destroy it.

## Why this role exists

Prema wins by becoming the most trusted public voice on resolution risk. That's
a marketing problem at least as much as a technical one. You'd own everything
outward-facing: positioning, content, community, press, and the early customer
pipeline.

## What the work looks like (first ~6 months)

- **Launch the dataset and the accuracy page as public assets.** Positioning,
  landing page, launch posts, and the narrative around them: *here is every
  resolution since 2024, here is how well our score predicts trouble — judge us
  openly.*
- **Data-driven content.** Turn the dataset into posts and threads: how dispute
  rates are shifting, anatomies of famous contested markets, which wording
  patterns blow up. The founder supplies the raw findings; you make them land.
- **Rapid response during live disputes.** When a large market gets contested,
  publish a clear neutral summary within hours. Those are the moments when
  traders, journalists and venues are all searching — the single best growth
  lever we have.
- **Community presence.** Be credible where the audience lives: X, UMA's
  governance forum, Polymarket and Kalshi trader communities, relevant Discords.
- **Press relationships.** The Defiant, CoinDesk, Blockworks, and increasingly
  the WSJ and Bloomberg, now cover disputed markets regularly. Make Prema the
  source they quote.
- **Early pipeline.** Run the waitlist for the risk-score API, warm up
  automated-trading operators and market makers (our first paying segment), and
  support design-partner conversations.
- **Later:** sales collateral, pricing pages, venue-facing materials, and the
  marketing sections of grant and funding applications.

## The part that's genuinely hard

A concrete example of the standard, so you can judge the work honestly.

We recently measured that the *rate* of disputes on Polymarket fell by about
26× after a rule change in September 2025 — while the *absolute number* of
disputes went **up**, because trading volume grew even faster.

Both facts are true. Publishing either one alone produces a false picture:

- *"Disputes are exploding"* — defensible on counts, false on rates
- *"Disputes have been solved"* — defensible on rates, false on counts

A hostile reader will check. Getting this right is most of the job, and getting
it wrong once — in a brand whose entire proposition is neutrality and
accuracy — costs more than a month of good posts earns.

If that sounds like an interesting constraint rather than an annoying one, this
role probably suits you.

## What this role is not
